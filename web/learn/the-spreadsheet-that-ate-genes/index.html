The Spreadsheet That Ate Genes

Catch values corrupted by spreadsheet software.

A career-making cancer result, one shared spreadsheet, and 1,400 genes. Something got eaten in transit.

Play this case →

The story

At the Rao Lab in Bengaluru, postdoc Kavya Menon spent eighteen months growing tumour organoids and sequencing them before and after a new drug. The analysis pipeline spat out a clean table of 1,400 genes, and her collaborator in Leeds, Dr. Tom Hartley, opened it, tidied it up and sent it back for the paper's supplement.

The journal's data editor has now put the submission on hold. Her email is short: 'Several entries in your supplementary table do not correspond to anything in the human genome. Please explain before we proceed.' The editor has not said which entries.

Kavya cannot face telling Professor Rao that a year and a half of work is stuck. She has handed you the exact file Tom sent back. Work out what the editor saw, and whether it can be fixed, before the lab meeting on Friday morning.

What you'll practise

  • reading a column's real contents
  • nunique() against row counts
  • sorting to surface oddities

The fault lives in one column. Summary statistics, value counts and a histogram will get you there. You write real pandas, NumPy and scikit-learn code in a notebook that runs in your browser. Nothing to install.

The data

A differential-expression table from an RNA-seq experiment: one row per gene, treated vs untreated tumour organoids. It went through a collaborator's hands before submission, and something in it no longer holds up. Find it. 1400 rows, loaded as df.

ColumnWhat it holds
gene_idstring, stable Ensembl-style gene identifier
gene_symbolstring, human gene symbol
base_meanfloat, mean normalised read count across samples
log2_fold_changefloat, log2 of treated / untreated expression
lfc_sefloat, standard error of the log2 fold change
p_valuefloat, Wald test p-value
padjfloat, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value
